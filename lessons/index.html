Debits and Credits

Lecture 1
Lesson 1 · Concept

Debit means left. Credit means right.

The terms debit (Dr.) and credit (Cr.) mean left and right. They do not mean increase or decrease — they describe where a company makes entries in the recording process.

  • An entry on the left side of an account is a debit to the account.
  • An entry on the right side is a credit to the account.
  • An account shows a debit balance if total debits exceed total credits.
  • An account shows a credit balance if total credits exceed total debits.

Under the universally used double-entry accounting system, every transaction is recorded with equal debits and credits — so the sum of all debits must always equal the sum of all credits. That equality is a built-in check on the accuracy of the records. Positioning debits on the left and credits on the right is simply the custom — like driving on the right-hand side of the road, the rule applies to all accounts.

Financial Statements & Ownership Structure

Lecture 3
Lesson · Concept

How the statements relate

The stockholders’ equity section of the balance sheet reports common stock and retained earnings. The income statement reports revenues and expenses. The retained earnings statement reports net income or loss and dividends.

Because a company transfers dividends, revenues, and expenses to retained earnings at the end of the period, a change in any one of these three items affects stockholders’ equity.

Analyze & Record Business Transactions

2
Learning Objective
Record and summarize accounting transactions.

Every number in a company’s books begins as a real-world event. This section builds the habit at the heart of accounting: look at what happened, decide whether it changed the company’s financial position, and — if it did — record it so the accounting equation always stays in balance.

The Road Ahead

The Accounting Cycle

The recording process moves through nine steps, in order, every period. This course opens at the very first one — analyzing transactions. Tap any step to see what it does.

1
The Core Idea

What Counts as a Transaction?

Transactions are a business’s economic events that accountants record. Companies do plenty of other things — hiring, answering e-mail, meeting clients — but only events that change assets, liabilities, or stockholders’ equity get recorded. Transactions come in two kinds.

External
Company ↔ outsiders

Economic events between the company and an outside party.

→Buying equipment from a supplier →Paying monthly rent to a landlord →Selling a service to a customer
Internal
Inside the company

Economic events that occur entirely within one company.

→Using up cleaning supplies →Consuming cooking materials in the kitchen →Depreciating equipment over time

The Rule That Keeps It Balanced

Every Transaction Has a Dual Effect

Recording a transaction always touches at least two items. If one asset increases, something else must move to keep Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity. When an asset increases, exactly one of these must happen:

An asset increases
↓
Decrease another asset
↑
Increase a liability
↑
Increase stockholders’ equity
Worked example

A company buys a $10,000 piece of equipment, paying $6,000 in cash now and agreeing to pay the $4,000 balance in 30 days.

Equipment +$10,000 = Cash −$6,000 + Accounts Payable +$4,000
Net change in assets: +$10,000 − $6,000 = +$4,000, matched by the +$4,000 liability. Still in balance.

The Recording Process

Although you could enter transactions straight into the accounts, almost no business does. Every transaction moves through three basic steps — the heart of the accounting cycle. Follow one purchase all the way through.

Overview

Three Steps, Every Transaction

The first step — analyze — you met in the section intro. The other two, journalize and post, are what this lecture is about.

1 Analyze

Read the source document and decide which accounts change, and whether each is debited or credited.

2 Journalize

Record the transaction in the general journal as equal debits and credits, in date order.

3 Post

Transfer each journal amount into the individual ledger account it affects.

Takeaway

Journal First, Ledger Second

The journal captures the whole transaction in one place, in the order it happened. The ledger takes those same debits and credits and re-files them by account, so you can read a running balance. Nothing new is invented in the ledger — it is the journal, re-sorted.

The Journal

Companies record transactions in chronological order, so the journal is called the book of original entry. Here you’ll learn its exact form — then build an entry yourself.

Why It Matters

What the Journal Does

Every company keeps at least one general journal — spaces for dates, account titles and explanations, and two amount columns. It makes three contributions to the recording process:

Complete effects

It discloses the full effect of a transaction in one place — both sides, together.

Chronological record

It provides a date-ordered history of everything the business did.

Catches errors

Debits and credits sit side by side, so an out-of-balance entry is easy to spot and locate.

Accounting Matters
Working on the Chain Gang

A ledger doesn’t have to live in one company’s books. Blockchain — the technology behind digital currencies like bitcoin — is a decentralized, digital ledger shared across a peer-to-peer network. Transactions are grouped into time-stamped “blocks” linked to the block before, and once recorded they can’t be altered retroactively.

Enhanced security, near real-time settlement, and lower fraud risk are pushing this into accounting. As organizations adopt it across many use cases, financial-reporting systems — and the profession itself — will adapt from record-keeper toward advisor.

How Many Accounts?

It Depends on the Business

How many accounts a company keeps depends on how much detail management wants. One company might use a single Utilities Expense account; another splits it into gas, electricity, and water.

A small business may manage on 20 to 30 accounts, while a giant like Anheuser-Busch InBev needs thousands to track worldwide activities. Numbers identify each account’s place in the ledger.

The Procedure

Three Steps to Build It

1. List balances

Write each account title and its ledger balance in the correct column.

2. Total the columns

Add the debit column and the credit column separately.

3. Prove equality

Confirm the two totals agree. If they don’t, there is an error to find.

Interactive · Know Its Limits

Errors a Trial Balance Won’t Catch

The columns can agree even when the books are wrong. As long as equal debits and credits were posted — even to the wrong account or amount — the totals still match. Tap each to see why it slips through.

If a whole transaction is left out, both its debit and its credit are missing. The columns stay equal because nothing was added to either side — yet the books are incomplete.
If an entire journal entry is never posted, both sides are omitted from the ledger together. The trial balance still balances, but two accounts are understated.
Posting the same entry twice adds an equal debit and credit a second time. The columns still agree — both totals are simply overstated by the same amount.
Debiting or crediting the wrong account still records equal debits and credits. The totals match, but the amounts sit in the wrong places.
If a debit and its matching credit are both recorded at the same wrong amount, the errors cancel. The columns balance despite both being incorrect.
In short: as long as a company posts equal debits and credits — even to the wrong account or in the wrong amount — the total debits will still equal the total credits.
