What it is

A system for turning events into information

Financial accounting captures a company's economic events — sales, purchases, borrowing, investing — and reports them in financial statements that outsiders can trust and compare.

Why it matters

Decisions ride on the numbers

Module 1 · Lesson 1

Debits & Credits

The grammar of the double-entry system · ~15 min
0%

Before you can read a single financial statement, you have to speak the language of the ledger — and its entire grammar rests on two words.

"Debit" and "credit" are not about good news or bad news. They are simply directions: left and right. Learn to hear them that way and accounting stops being memorization.

1The core idea

Debit means left. Credit means right.

The terms debit (abbreviated Dr.) and credit (abbreviated Cr.) mean left and right, respectively. They do not mean increase or decrease. They describe where a company records an amount in an account.

L
An entry on the left side of an account is a debit to the account.
R
An entry on the right side is a credit to the account.
▸
An account shows a debit balance when total debits exceed total credits.
◂
An account shows a credit balance when total credits exceed total debits.
2Why left and right?

A custom that became the rule

There is nothing natural about putting debits on the left — it is an arbitrary convention, like driving on the right-hand side of the road. But it is now the rule, applied to every account, so entries can always be combined and checked the same way.

3The part students trip on

Increase or decrease depends on the account

A debit only raises a balance for some accounts. Whether a debit is an increase or a decrease depends entirely on the type of account you are recording in:

+
Assets, expenses, and dividends increase with debits (left) and decrease with credits.
+
Liabilities, revenues, and stockholders' equity (Common Stock, Retained Earnings) increase with credits (right) and decrease with debits.

4The double-entry system

Every transaction has two sides

Each transaction is recorded with equal amounts on both sides — debit one account, credit another for the same amount. Buy equipment with cash and Equipment is debited while Cash is credited. This is double-entry accounting, and that built-in equality is also what lets the system prove itself.

5The self-proving check

Total debits must equal total credits

If every transaction is recorded with equal debits and credits, then across the entire ledger the sum of all debits must equal the sum of all credits. That equality is the mechanical backbone of the trial balance and the reason the system can catch its own arithmetic errors.

”

Professor's takeaway

Always classify the account first, decide whether it went up or down, and then — and only then — choose debit or credit. Balancing proves the arithmetic, not the judgment: you can debit the wrong account and still balance.

The Accounting Equation

Why the books must always balance · ~15 min
0%

Master one equation and you hold the whole of accounting in a single line.

In a double-entry system, for every debit there must be a credit — and vice versa. Follow that to its conclusion and you arrive at the equation the entire system exists to keep true.

1The core identity

Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders' Equity

Assets are the resources the company controls; liabilities are what it owes creditors; stockholders' equity is the owners' residual claim — what is left after creditors are paid. Because every transaction posts equal debits and credits, the two sides can never drift out of balance.

Assetsresources = Liabilitiesowed + Equityowners' claim

Where stockholders' equity comes from

Sign a note to buy equipment and assets and liabilities rise together. When debits equal credits, the equation balances automatically.

The accounting equation is the master control on the entire ledger. If it will not balance, a debit and a credit somewhere disagree — find that error before you trust a single statement.

Module 1 · Lesson 3

Financial Statements

How the statements connect & ownership structure · ~15 min
0%

Three statements, one story — and a single account stitches them together.

The income statement, the retained earnings statement, and the balance sheet are not three separate reports. They are three views of the same period, linked by numbers that flow from one into the next.

1The three connected statements

What each statement reports

The income statement reports revenues and expenses; their difference is net income. The retained earnings statement runs Beginning RE + Net income − Dividends = Ending RE. The balance sheet's equity section then reports Common Stock and Retained Earnings.

2Follow the money

One number moves through all three

Net income from the income statement flows into the retained earnings statement. Ending retained earnings then flows into the equity section of the balance sheet. Common Stock reflects investments by stockholders; Retained Earnings reflects net income retained in the business. See it move on the Fig 2.4 tab.

3Two kinds of account

Temporary vs. permanent

Revenues, expenses, and dividends are nominal (temporary) accounts — they measure a single period and are closed into a real (permanent) account at period-end. Assets, liabilities, common stock, and retained earnings are permanent accounts whose balances carry into the next period. That is why each period's revenue and expense accounts start again at zero.

T
Temporary (nominal): Revenues, Expenses, Dividends — reset each period.
P
Permanent (real): Assets, Liabilities, Common Stock, Retained Earnings — carry forward.
4Who owns the business?

Ownership structure sets the account names

Same economics, different equity labels: a corporation uses Common Stock, Dividends, and Retained Earnings; a proprietorship or partnership uses Owner's Capital and Owner's Drawings. The Practice tab drills the full mapping with Illustration 2.5.

”

Professor's takeaway

Learn to follow one number as it travels. The net income you compute on the income statement is the same net income that raises retained earnings and reappears in equity on the balance sheet. If those figures do not tie, the statements are not finished.

Analyze & Record Business Transactions

2
Learning Objective
Record and summarize accounting transactions.

What counts as a transaction?

Business transactions are a business's economic events recorded by accountants. They may be external or internal.

External Between the company & an outsider

Economic events between the company and an outside enterprise. For Campus Pizza: buying cooking equipment from a supplier, paying monthly rent to the landlord, and selling pizzas to customers.

Internal Entirely within the company

Economic events that occur entirely inside one company. For Campus Pizza, the use of cooking and cleaning supplies is an internal transaction.

Not every event is a transaction

Companies do many things that are not business transactions — some may lead to one later, but on their own they change nothing to record.

Hiring employees Answering e-mails Talking with customers Placing an order

The company must analyze each event against one test — and record it only if it passes.

The Journal

The book of original entry · journalizing · ~10 min

Companies record every transaction in chronological order — which is why the journal is called the book of original entry.

For each transaction, the journal shows the debit and credit effects on specific accounts, all in one place.

§The general journal

Every company keeps one

Companies use various journals, but every company has the most basic form — a general journal. It has spaces for dates, account titles and explanations, references, and two amount columns (debit and credit). Whenever we say "journal," we mean the general journal.

Why the journal matters

1
It discloses in one place the complete effects of a transaction.
2
It provides a chronological record of transactions.
3
It helps prevent or locate errors — the debit and credit amounts of each entry can be compared at a glance.
§Journalizing

What a complete entry contains

Entering transaction data in the journal is called journalizing. Companies make a separate entry for each transaction, and a complete entry has three parts:

1
The date of the transaction.
2
The accounts and amounts to be debited and credited.
3
A brief explanation of the transaction.

The Trial Balance

Prove that total debits equal total credits · ~9 min

A trial balance is simply a list of every account and its balance at a point in time.

Companies prepare one at the end of a period to prove that, after posting, total debits still equal total credits.

What it is & what it proves

The trial balance lists accounts in ledger order — debit balances in the left column, credit balances in the right — and the two column totals must agree. That equality is the mathematical proof of double-entry after posting; it also helps surface errors and feeds the financial statements. Three steps:

1
List the account titles and their balances.
2
Total the debit and credit columns.
3
Prove the equality of the two columns.

    Adjusting Entries

    3
    Learning Objective
    Identify and prepare adjusting entries.

    Why adjust at all?

    Adjusting entries make sure revenues are recorded in the period services are performed and expenses in the period they are incurred — the revenue recognition and expense recognition principles. They let the balance sheet report the right assets, liabilities, and equity at the statement date, and the income statement report the proper revenues and expenses.

    Why the trial balance isn't ready

    The initial trial balance may not be up to date or complete, for three reasons:

    1
    Not recorded daily

    Some events aren't recorded as they happen because it isn't efficient.

    e.g. supplies used up · salaries earned by employees
    2
    Expire with time

    Some costs expire with the passage of time, not through daily transactions.

    e.g. depreciation · rent · insurance
    3
    Unrecorded items

    Some items simply haven't been recorded yet.

    e.g. a utility bill that arrives next period

    Which reason needs an adjustment?

    Match each event to the reason it slipped through. Tap your answer.

    Two families of adjustment

    Every adjusting entry is either a deferral or an accrual — the two lessons coming next.

    Deferrals

    Cash already changed hands, but the revenue or expense belongs to this period. Examples: prepaid expenses and unearned revenues.

    Accruals

    The revenue or expense happened, but no cash or entry yet. Examples: accrued revenues and accrued expenses.

    i
    Adjustments are prepared every time a company reports — and usually by a skilled accountant. Companies often prepare them after the balance sheet date but date the entries as of the balance sheet date.

    Deferrals vs. accruals — it's all about timing

    The only thing that separates the two classes is when the cash moves relative to the revenue or expense it belongs to.

    DeferralsCash → Event

    Cash first. You've already paid or been paid; recognition of the expense or revenue is deferred to a later period as the benefit is used up or earned.

    AccrualsEvent → Cash

    Cash later. The service or cost has already happened, but nothing has been recorded and no cash has changed hands yet — so it must be accrued now.

    Module 3 · Lesson 2

    Adjusting Entries for Deferrals

    2.3
    Section
    Prepaid expenses & unearned revenues — cash that arrived early.

        Converting is just undoing cash timing: add what you earned but haven't collected, remove what you collected but haven't earned — and mirror it for expenses. The receivables, payables, and prepayments are the bridge between the two bases.

        Theoretical Weaknesses of the Cash Basis

        ?
        The big question
        "Cash is king" — so why does GAAP require the accrual basis?

        The cash basis reports exactly when cash comes in and goes out — concrete, tangible. Many people say "cash is the real bottom line." So what's the merit of accrual accounting?

        Two reasons accrual wins

        The economy runs on credit

        Today's economy is driven far more by credit transactions than by cash. Only the accrual basis recognizes all aspects of the credit phenomenon — the sale made on account, the expense incurred but unpaid.

        Decision-makers want to predict cash flows

        Investors and creditors seek timely information about future cash flows. Accrual accounting reports the cash inflows and outflows tied to earnings as soon as they can be estimated with reasonable certainty.

        Receivables & payables are forecasters

        A receivable predicts a future cash inflow; a payable predicts a future cash outflow. By recording transactions when they occur — not when the cash finally moves — accrual-basis accounting actually helps you predict future cash flows better than the cash basis does.

        i
        Ironically, the basis that ignores today's cash timing (accrual) is the one that best forecasts tomorrow's cash.

        Professor's takeaway

        Cash is king — which is exactly why accrual accounting matters. Because it captures receivables and payables the moment they arise, the accrual basis gives investors the earliest, clearest read on the cash a business will actually generate.

        ?
        The big question
        Net income is the most-watched number in business. What exactly does it measure — and why does the market move on it?

        Welcome to Course 2. In Course 1 you learned how the numbers get recorded. Now we zoom in on the statement those numbers feed — the income statement — and the rules for measuring, presenting, and recognizing income.

        What is the income statement?

        One of the primary financial statements. It measures a company's financial results — its profitability — over a period of time. The bottom line, net income, is measured as:

        =
        Net income = all revenues and gains in a period − the expenses and losses incurred to generate those revenues. The higher the net income, the more positive the assessment of performance for the period.

        Why the market watches it

        The objective of financial reporting is information that helps predict future cash flows. Revenues and expenses capture the effects of past transactions — and past results are often the best guide to future operating results.
